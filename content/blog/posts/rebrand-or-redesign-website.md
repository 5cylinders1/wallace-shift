Your website no longer feels right for the business. Perhaps the company now serves different customers. Perhaps the offer is unchanged, but the site is hard to use. Or the logo looks dated while the services are explained accurately.

Those are different problems. Calling all of them a rebrand can turn a focused website improvement into a larger project. Calling a change in audience or positioning a redesign can leave the team decorating a message it has not resolved.

Use this guide to decide what has changed, what customers need to understand, and which parts of the business presentation need attention. The result should be a defined brief, not an automatic recommendation to replace everything.

## Use working definitions before discussing scope

Teams use these terms differently, so agree on the work behind each label.

| Type of work | Primary question |
| --- | --- |
| Brand clarification or rebrand | Who are we for, what do we promise, and how should people recognize us? |
| Visual identity refresh | How should the existing business identity be presented consistently? |
| Website redesign | How should the site explain the offer and help people complete relevant tasks? |
| Focused website repair | Which specific information or interaction needs fixing? |

These are practical distinctions for scoping a project, not rigid industry categories. A redesign can include new writing. A rebrand can preserve the existing name. A visual refresh can leave the positioning and website structure largely intact.

Ask a prospective partner to list the decisions and deliverables included in its proposal. “Rebrand” might mean a logo package to one provider and audience research, messaging, identity, and rollout planning to another.

## Look for a change in the business, not only its appearance

Start with what is true today that was not true when the current identity and website were created.

Have you changed the customers you want to serve, the service model, or the work you no longer offer? Has a merger or new business structure created confusion? Does the name strongly suggest an offer you have moved beyond? Do customers consistently misunderstand the promise?

Record evidence: recent sales conversations, repeated questions, customer descriptions, and examples of mismatched inquiries. Separate those observations from an internal preference for a different color or typeface.

The Design Council's Double Diamond framework begins with understanding the problem and defining the challenge before developing and testing solutions. That sequence is useful here: investigate the mismatch before commissioning its visual treatment. [Design Council's Double Diamond](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/the-double-diamond/).

## Signs the message needs work

A brand or messaging project may deserve attention when the business cannot consistently explain its intended customer, its offer, or the reason someone should choose it.

For example, sales may describe a specialist service while the website presents a general provider. Different proposals may use incompatible promises. Customers may recognize the business for work it no longer wants to sell.

Start by resolving the substance:

- Who is the primary customer, and who is outside the intended scope?
- Which problem does the business take responsibility for?
- What does the service actually include and exclude?
- Which claims can the business support with evidence?
- Which aspects of the existing identity help customers recognize it?

A new tagline cannot settle unanswered service decisions. If the offer itself is still changing, document those decisions before asking a designer to make every page consistent with it.

## Signs the website needs work

A redesign may be the better focus when the business message is sound but the site prevents people from finding information or completing a task.

Examples include a confusing service structure, an editing system the team cannot maintain, mobile layouts that obstruct the contact flow, or a booking integration that no longer fits the process.

These findings do not automatically require a new name or logo. A focused repair may also be enough. Determine which limitations can be corrected in the existing site and which require broader structural work.

Our [website redesign strategy guide](/blog/website-redesign-strategy-what-to-prioritize-first) covers the planning and launch work after that decision. Keep this earlier conversation focused on what the redesign must communicate and enable.

## Three hypothetical decisions

### The offer is stable, but finding it is difficult

A fictional electrician still serves the same homeowners with the same services. The site puts essential details inside image-based brochures and makes it difficult to request a visit on a phone.

The initial brief should focus on accessible service information and the request journey. Preserve recognizable identity elements unless there is separate evidence that they need changing. The business does not need to redefine its audience to repair its website.

### The business has moved to a different market

A fictional consultancy previously sold one-off training to individuals and now provides ongoing programs for employers. Its language, examples, and process still describe individual bookings.

Clarifying the offer and intended buyer should come before the detailed page design. The team needs to decide how employer engagements work, what can be promised, and which evidence is relevant. A redesign can then express the agreed direction. A name change remains a separate decision.

### The identity is inconsistent, but the proposition is clear

A fictional maintenance company uses three versions of its logo and conflicting colors across its website, proposals, and vehicles. Customers understand the service, and the team wants to remain in the same market.

A visual identity refresh and a simple usage guide may address the inconsistency. Scope the affected website components without assuming every page, URL, and system needs replacement.

These examples illustrate decisions, not measured customer outcomes.

## Test the message before designing every page

Write a short proposed explanation of the audience, service, and next step. Put it beside the current explanation and identify the important change in meaning.

Ask a small set of relevant people to describe who the business serves, what it provides, and what they would do next. Avoid asking only whether they like it. A preferred slogan may still leave the service unclear.

Look for misunderstandings that matter to a buying decision. Do people expect a different service, audience, level of involvement, or price model? Record disagreement rather than averaging it into a preference score.

This feedback helps refine the brief; it does not establish market demand or guarantee sales. If the change concerns an untested offer, evaluate that business assumption separately from the design project.

## Preserve recognition while planning the change

Inventory where the current identity appears: website, proposals, onboarding messages, invoices, business profiles, vehicles, documents, and email signatures. Decide which items must change together and which can follow a planned transition.

If existing customers need an explanation, specify what changed and what remains relevant to their service. Give staff consistent wording so the new website and the next conversation do not tell different stories.

A name change also has search presentation implications. Google generates site names automatically and considers homepage information and other signals; its documentation recommends consistent naming and `WebSite` structured data to indicate a preference. Updating those signals does not guarantee an immediate chosen display. [Google's site-name guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/site-names).

Confirm naming rights and other applicable requirements with qualified advisers when the proposed change requires that work. Website availability alone is not the whole naming decision.

## Treat a domain move as a separate project decision

Changing the visual identity or message does not necessarily require a new domain. Keep that decision explicit rather than allowing it to become an assumed part of the rebrand.

If URLs change, the project needs a migration plan, including relevant redirects and verification. Google advises separating major site changes where possible and notes that significant moves can produce ranking fluctuations while pages are processed. A domain change, platform replacement, and visual redesign do not have to be one simultaneous launch. [Google's site-move guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes).

Have the responsible implementer explain how old links, email addresses, customer communications, and connected services will be handled. Do not promise that a new name or domain will improve search rankings.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can we redesign without rebranding?

Yes. You can improve structure, content presentation, usability, and integrations while retaining the business identity. Document the elements to preserve so the design brief is clear.

### Does a rebrand mean changing the business name?

No. The work may focus on audience, message, or visual identity while keeping the name. Treat naming as a decision with its own evidence, implications, and checks.

### Should we do both projects together?

They can share planning when both are justified. Resolve foundational audience and offer decisions before detailed page work depends on them. Consider separate release stages when combining major technical changes would make verification and recovery harder.
