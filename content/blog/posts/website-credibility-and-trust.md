A potential customer sees a confident headline, a neat logo, and a row of impressive-looking badges. They still have practical questions: Who will do the work? Does this business handle a project like mine? What do those badges mean? Will someone respond if I get in touch?

Website credibility comes from giving people enough clear, relevant information to make that judgment. Design determines how well you present it. The two belong together, but they are different jobs.

This guide helps you inspect the evidence behind your website, its presentation, and the promises its interactions make. It is not a scoring system that predicts trust or sales. Use the worksheet below to replace vague concerns with specific improvements.

## Separate appearance, evidence, and behavior

When someone says a website looks unprofessional, ask what they noticed. Their answer may reveal three different problems.

| Review area | A useful question |
| --- | --- |
| Appearance | Can I read the page and recognize its structure without unnecessary effort? |
| Evidence | Can I judge who is responsible and whether the business can support its claims? |
| Behavior | Do links, forms, and next steps work as described? |

A page can look coherent while offering little evidence. A capable business can have strong evidence hidden in confusing pages. A well-written contact form can still fail to deliver a request.

Keep these findings separate. “The headings have inconsistent spacing” suggests a presentation repair. “I cannot find who provides the service” suggests missing information. Neither finding, on its own, proves that the website is losing customers.

## Show who is responsible for the work

Write an About page that helps a buyer understand the actual business. Include the people or team involved, their roles, relevant experience, and the way the work is delivered. Explain subcontracting or partnerships accurately when those arrangements affect the customer's experience.

A solo business does not need to sound like a large agency. A clear account of who plans, builds, reviews, and supports the work can be more informative than an undefined “team of experts.” Use credentials you can substantiate and make their relevance clear.

Google's people-first content guidance asks site owners to consider clear sourcing, evidence of expertise, and background about the author or publisher. That is a useful editorial check; an About page is not a promise of improved rankings. [Google's helpful-content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

Provide a suitable way to contact the business and accurate service-area information. A home-based operator does not need to publish a private residential address simply to fill a design template. Explain how customers can reach you and where you work without inventing a location or office.

## Replace broad claims with inspectable evidence

Start with the strongest statements on the homepage and service pages. For each one, ask what a reader could examine to judge it.

“We understand complicated projects” is difficult to assess. A clearly labeled example describing the constraint, decisions, and delivered work gives someone more to consider. “We keep you informed” becomes more concrete when the service explanation names the review points and communication process you actually provide.

Match the evidence to the claim:

- Use a relevant project example to explain how a requirement was handled.
- Use a genuine customer quotation to describe that customer's experience.
- Link a qualification to its issuing organization when verification is available.
- Explain the method and time period behind a reported result.
- Label demonstration work, prototypes, and hypothetical examples clearly.

Do not turn an unrelated achievement into evidence for a service you have never delivered. If you have no measured results, describe the work and its limitations accurately. A useful process explanation does not need a fabricated percentage.

Keep permission, the original wording, and the source with any testimonial you plan to publish. Review quotes when services or staff change so an old statement does not describe an offer that no longer exists.

## Put evidence where the question arises

A full portfolio can help someone explore your work. It should not be the only place a visitor encounters supporting information.

On a service page, identify the concern that needs answering. A customer hiring someone for a complex installation may want to understand planning and handover. A customer comparing a routine service may need scope, availability, and communication details. Choose the example that addresses the relevant concern.

Keep a short explanation beside the claim and link to detail when useful. Avoid adding a long wall of testimonials to every page. Repetition is not a substitute for relevance.

For a new business, an honest sample deliverable, a clearly labeled demonstration, or a detailed explanation of the process can provide something to assess. Describe what the example demonstrates and what it does not establish.

## Make presentation consistent and usable

Professional presentation does not require a particular visual style. Review whether the site uses a coherent type hierarchy, readable text, clear spacing, relevant images, and predictable navigation. Check the actual pages on a phone and a larger screen.

Repeated controls should remain recognizable. A booking link in the header should not appear to become a different action as someone moves between pages. W3C's guidance on consistent identification addresses recognizing recurring components with the same function; it does not require every link with a related purpose to use identical wording. [W3C's consistent-identification guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/consistent-identification.html).

Inspect contact forms with the same care as the homepage. W3C recommends labels that identify form controls and are associated with them. A label that disappears when someone types can leave the purpose of a field unclear. [W3C's labeling guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/labels/).

Ask someone unfamiliar with the site to find a service, explain what it includes, and locate the next step. Record where they pause and what they misunderstand. Treat this as practical feedback, not a representative study of every customer.

## Check the promises made by interactions

“Book now,” “Request availability,” and “Ask for a quote” describe different outcomes. A visitor should know whether they are reserving something or starting a conversation.

After a form submission, explain what happened and what comes next. If the request failed, give a clear route to correction. W3C's notification guidance covers understandable error feedback and confirmation of successful submission. [W3C's form-notification guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/notifications/).

Then verify the business side. Did the message arrive? Does somebody own it? Is the stated response expectation realistic during weekends or holidays?

## A hypothetical credibility repair

Imagine a fictional property-maintenance business whose site says “Complete property care.” It has attractive photographs but no service area, no explanation of what “complete” includes, and a button labeled “Get started.”

The first revision could explain the actual offer: routine grounds maintenance for small commercial properties in a defined area. A service page could describe included tasks, exclusions, and how a site assessment works. The button could become “Request a site assessment” if that is what the team provides.

An available project example could explain the property's requirements and the maintenance schedule delivered. If the photographs are stock images, the business should not present them as completed projects.

The visual refresh can then organize that information. The intended improvement is a more understandable and supportable presentation. Whether it produces more suitable inquiries requires observation after the changes.

Classify each finding as **missing information**, **unsupported claim**, **presentation problem**, or **broken behavior**. Correct unsupported statements and broken interactions first. Then organize the missing information and presentation work around the customer's actual questions.

Do not add the findings into a universal “trust score.” A missing review and a nonfunctional contact form are different problems with different consequences.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a template website look professional?

Yes. The useful questions are whether it presents accurate information clearly and supports the required customer tasks. A template with coherent content and working interactions can serve a business well. Custom design does not establish credibility by itself.

### What if we do not have testimonials yet?

Explain who does the work, show relevant experience you can substantiate, and provide a useful account of the process. Clearly labeled samples can demonstrate particular skills. Add genuine feedback when it becomes available instead of making a demonstration look like client work.

### Should we redesign the whole site to fix credibility problems?

Only if the existing structure prevents the needed improvements. Missing details, unsupported claims, inconsistent buttons, and failed forms may be repairable individually.
