The right website approach depends on what your business needs the site to do. A template may provide an appropriate starting structure. Custom design may be worthwhile when your content, brand, or customer journey needs something materially different.

The choice is rarely “everything from scratch” versus “an untouched template.” A project can combine original design with an established content system, or adapt a template while adding a narrowly scoped integration.

Before comparing prices, ask what each provider means by custom. Then evaluate the proposed approach against the same requirements.

## Separate design, content, and functionality

These decisions affect each other, but they are not interchangeable:

| Layer | What you are deciding | A possible approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Content | What information the site needs and how it is organized | Original writing and a structure based on customer questions |
| Visual design | How pages, components, and interactions look and behave | Adapted theme, original design, or a mixture |
| Content management | How staff add and maintain information | Existing CMS, hosted editor, or a specifically built workflow |
| Business functionality | How the site processes requests or connects to systems | Existing service, integration, or custom application work |

An original visual design does not require an original database or administrative system. Conversely, a site using a familiar visual layout may need substantial work behind the scenes to support permissions, records, or integrations.

MDN distinguishes browser-side presentation and interaction from server-side work such as processing requests and generating responses using stored data. That is a useful reason to ask providers to separate interface work from backend responsibilities in the proposal. [MDN's introduction to server-side development](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Extensions/Server-side/First_steps/Introduction).

“Custom” should identify what is tailored, not simply sound more valuable.

## When a template is a reasonable starting point

A template deserves consideration when its structure fits the content, its editing tools suit your staff, and its supported features meet the essential requirements.

For example, a business may need an explanation of its services, a team introduction, a project gallery, and an inquiry form. If a suitable starting point presents that information clearly, the important work may be writing, choosing real photographs, and configuring the customer handoff.

A template does not have to remain visually untouched. WordPress's Site Editor, when used with a block theme, supports changes to site-wide styles, templates, and areas such as headers and footers. The available editing model depends on the platform and theme. [WordPress Site Editor documentation](https://wordpress.org/documentation/article/site-editor/).

Check the actual template with your content. A demonstration containing three short service names and carefully cropped photographs may behave differently with your longer explanations and mixed image sizes.

Also examine what customization involves. A small visual adjustment and replacing the template's central navigation are different requests. Ask which changes are straightforward, which require development, and which would make the starting point a poor fit.

## When custom design may be justified

Custom design becomes more compelling when you can name a requirement that available layouts handle poorly.

Perhaps customers must compare services across several meaningful criteria. Perhaps your project stories need drawings, photographs, and technical explanations in a sequence that a standard gallery cannot express clearly. Perhaps several audience groups need different routes through a shared body of information.

Write the requirement in terms of the reader's task. “Customers need to compare installation options without losing track of the differences” gives a designer something to solve. “We want something nobody has seen before” provides less direction.

Distinctive art direction can also be a legitimate goal. Decide which elements matter: typography, photography, layout, motion, or a particular storytelling approach. Treat those choices as a deliberate investment in presentation, without attaching an unsupported promise of additional sales.

Custom work should have limits. Reusing suitable components and established tools can still be part of a tailored project. Ask why each bespoke element is necessary and how it will be maintained.

## Do not confuse a design limitation with a platform limitation

Changing a layout will not automatically solve an unsuitable editing workflow or missing business capability. Identify where the constraint lives before replacing the whole site.

For each difficult requirement, ask:

1. Is the information missing or poorly organized?
2. Is the current layout making it hard to understand?
3. Does the editor prevent staff from maintaining it properly?
4. Does a required operation depend on functionality the platform cannot provide?

A clearer service comparison might need content and component work. A customer portal with private job records introduces separate questions about identity, access, data, and ongoing operation. It should not appear in a website proposal as an incidental design feature.

Use the [website-or-web-app decision guide](/blog/web-app-vs-website) to separate public-facing pages from ongoing operational work.

When assessing integrations, demonstrate the whole task. Submitting a form is only one step if the business also needs a correctly assigned record in its CRM. Our [guide to reducing repetitive work](/blog/save-time-with-automation-stop-losing-hours-to-busy-work) explains how to map that handoff before choosing tools.

## Evaluate the result, including its quality checks

Neither the custom label nor the template label tells you whether the finished site meets your quality requirements. Ask how the actual pages and interactions will be assessed.

For accessibility, automated tools can help identify issues, but they cannot determine accessibility on their own; human evaluation remains necessary. Request a defined review process rather than treating a scanner score as the entire answer. [W3C guidance on evaluation tools](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/tools/selecting/).

For performance, agree on measurable limits and test conditions for representative pages. Google's web.dev describes a performance budget as limits on measures such as page weight or loading behavior that guide implementation decisions. Ask the provider to explain the budget appropriate to your site. [Performance budgets on web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/performance-budgets-101).

Your acceptance review should also include real content, working inquiry delivery, understandable error messages, and the editing tasks your staff will perform. A design preview is useful evidence of appearance, but it does not demonstrate all of those functions.

## Check editing and portability before committing

Ask a member of your team to try a representative edit in the proposed system. Adding a service, replacing an image, and changing a contact detail will reveal more than watching a developer move quickly through a demonstration.

Clarify which changes staff can make safely and which need help. Some businesses want broad layout control; others prefer a smaller set of structured fields that preserve the design. Choose deliberately.

Portability is a separate consideration. Wix, for example, states that a Wix site must operate on its infrastructure; using a domain registered elsewhere does not make the site independently hostable. This is a platform constraint, not a consequence of whether the visual design is custom. [Wix's hosting and export explanation](https://support.wix.com/en/article/exporting-or-embedding-your-wix-site-elsewhere).

Ask the same questions of any proposed platform: what can be exported, what would need rebuilding, which subscriptions are required, and what a different provider would need to take over. An acceptable constraint is one you understand and choose.

## Use this requirements worksheet

For each requirement, record why it matters and whether it is essential or optional. Ask each provider to add its proposed method and evidence, including any stated limitation.

| Requirement or check | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Explain our main services clearly | Test the proposed layout with our actual copy |
| Compare relevant options | Demonstrate how customers would make the comparison |
| Update projects without a developer | Have a staff member complete a trial edit |
| Deliver inquiries to the right place | Demonstrate receipt and assignment to an owner |
| Meet agreed quality requirements | Define the checks and record their results |
| Support a future handover | Explain access, export, and operating dependencies |

Add a “cost of meeting the requirement” note beside any gap. If a template needs extensive alterations to meet several essential requirements, compare that actual implementation scope with an original design. Do not compare an unmodified demo price with a completed custom project.

## A hypothetical worked example

Consider a commercial cleaning company with three services, approved copy, and a standard inquiry process. A template supports the content well, and the office manager can maintain the pages after a short demonstration. Original photographs and careful writing may provide the differentiation the business needs.

Now imagine the same company introduces a complex service-selection process. Customers need to compare facility types, scheduling constraints, and included tasks before requesting a consultation.

The team could test a custom comparison component within its existing platform. If that solves the problem, it may not need a completely new backend or site. If the requirement later becomes a private pricing and account-management system, that additional work needs its own scope.

The useful decision is the smallest coherent approach that meets the requirements and remains maintainable—not a permanent allegiance to either templates or custom development.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a template website still look distinctive?

Yes, depending on its customization options and the work applied to content, imagery, typography, and layout. Review an implementation using your material. Avoid judging only the untouched demonstration or assuming every element can be changed economically.

### Does custom mean every line of code is newly written?

No. Ask the provider to explain which parts are original and which use existing platforms, libraries, or components. The value lies in meeting the agreed requirements, not in maximizing the amount of code written for one project.

### Can we start with a template and change later?

Potentially, but the effort depends on the platform, content structure, integrations, and desired changes. Discuss likely future requirements now and document what would transfer. Do not assume that every starting point offers an inexpensive path to every future feature.

## Choose the approach after defining the work

Use the worksheet to identify essential requirements, then ask for a concrete approach and evidence that it fits. A template, custom design, or a focused combination may be the right answer.
