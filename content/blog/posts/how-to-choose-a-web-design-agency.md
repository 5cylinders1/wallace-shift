Choosing a web design agency becomes easier when you know what you are buying. A website proposal can cover strategy, writing, visual design, development, migration, training, and support—or only a few of those things.

A polished portfolio tells you something about presentation. Your decision also needs to account for how the work will be delivered, who will make decisions, and what your business can operate after launch.

Use the process below to turn a shortlist into a comparison you can explain. The worksheet near the end works in a document or spreadsheet; you do not need procurement software.

## Write a brief before collecting proposals

Describe the situation in plain language. Who uses the website? What should they understand or do? What is difficult today? What evidence do you have?

For example, “We need a modern site” leaves several interpretations open. “Customers cannot tell which service covers their problem, and requests arrive without a location” gives an agency something concrete to investigate.

Include these details in the brief:

- Your audiences, services, and most important customer actions.
- Existing pages, useful content, and integrations that must keep working.
- Content you can supply and content you need help creating.
- Who will approve the work and maintain the site afterward.
- Budget boundaries, genuine deadlines, and unresolved requirements.

Separate requirements from preferences. A working connection to your booking system may be essential. A particular animation might be optional. When both appear on one undifferentiated wish list, proposals become difficult to compare.

If you are still deciding whether a full rebuild is necessary, use our [website redesign planning guide](/blog/website-redesign-strategy-what-to-prioritize-first) to establish the problem first.

## Look for relevant evidence in the portfolio

Open a few completed sites and follow a realistic customer journey. Find a service, understand its scope, and locate the next step on a phone. A screenshot cannot show whether navigation, content, and forms work together.

Ask the agency to explain one project that resembles your challenge. Similarity might mean a complicated service offering, an editorial workflow, or an integration—not necessarily the same industry.

Find out what the agency actually contributed. Did it write the content, develop the site, or create the visual design? Was the example a client launch, a concept, or a collaboration? Clear attribution makes the evidence more useful.

When results are presented, ask how they were measured and what else changed. Increased inquiries after a launch could also coincide with advertising, a new offer, or a seasonal change. A credible explanation acknowledges those limits.

References can help you understand delivery. With the previous client's permission, ask about communication, unexpected changes, handover, and what happened when something needed fixing. Do not ask for their confidential commercial information.

## Meet the people responsible for the work

The sales conversation should explain how a project becomes a working site. Ask who will lead the work, who will implement it, and which responsibilities sit with partners or subcontractors.

There is nothing inherently wrong with a distributed team. What matters is whether the proposal accounts for coordination and accountability. You should know who answers a question that spans writing, design, and development.

Ask how decisions are recorded and where feedback goes. A useful answer describes reviewable milestones, a decision owner, and a method for resolving conflicting comments. “We communicate regularly” leaves too much unspecified.

Also discuss your side of the process. If your team cannot provide photographs or approve content before a fixed date, that dependency belongs in the plan. An agency cannot remove it merely by promising a quick launch.

To compare delivery arrangements, see [how an agency and a freelancer divide the work](/blog/web-design-agency-vs-freelancer).

## Ask what quality means in the scope

Words such as “responsive,” “accessible,” and “SEO-ready” need a practical definition. Request the work and evidence behind each claim.

For accessibility, ask what requirements guide the work, who evaluates it, and when issues are corrected. W3C recommends including accessibility throughout design and development and evaluating early as components become available. Treat it as a planned responsibility with review points. [W3C implementation guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning-and-managing/implement/).

For search work, identify what is included: content planning, page metadata, crawlability checks, migration work where needed, or ongoing analysis. Those are different activities. Google warns that nobody can guarantee a first-place ranking, so a ranking guarantee should prompt careful scrutiny. [Google's guidance on hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo).

For your business functions, request examples of acceptance checks. “An inquiry reaches the named inbox, records the requested service, and shows a confirmation” is something both sides can verify. “A high-converting website” does not define a deliverable by itself.

## Compare proposals on the same scope

Give each shortlisted agency the same core brief, then record where the proposals differ. You do not need identical methods, but you do need visible assumptions.

| Scope area | What to clarify |
| --- | --- |
| Content | Who interviews, writes, edits, supplies images, and approves? |
| Design | Which page types and components are included? How is feedback handled? |
| Development | Which platform, forms, and integrations will be implemented? |
| Existing site | Who inventories content and handles necessary migration work? |
| Verification | Which customer tasks and quality requirements will be checked? |
| Handover | Which access, documentation, and training will you receive? |
| Ongoing work | What is included, optional, recurring, or charged separately? |

Ask for exclusions as well as inclusions. A proposal may include an inquiry form but exclude a CRM integration. Another may assume you will upload all content. Neither is automatically unreasonable; both affect your workload and total cost.

Discuss changes before they happen. How is a new requirement estimated? Who approves it? Can the launch proceed without it? A written change process helps keep a useful idea from silently becoming an unexpected obligation.

## Check ownership, access, and the exit route

Ask who will control the domain registration, hosting account, website administration, analytics, and any source repository. Record account holders and recovery contacts without circulating passwords in the brief.

Access and intellectual property are separate questions. Clarify which deliverables you receive, which third-party products are licensed, and what happens to those licenses when the relationship ends. Request these terms in writing; do not infer ownership from a sales phrase such as “your website.”

Ask how another qualified provider could take over. What documentation exists? What can be exported? Which services would need replacing? A sensible answer may include limitations, especially on hosted platforms.

Finally, distinguish a warranty or correction period from ongoing support. Ask who handles an urgent failure, what support hours apply, and whether the agreement promises a response or a resolution. These commitments must come from the actual proposal.

## Use this agency evaluation worksheet

Copy the table and create one column per candidate. Use **confirmed**, **partly answered**, or **unresolved**, and attach evidence to each answer.

| Decision question | Evidence to record |
| --- | --- |
| Does the proposed scope address our main problem? | Relevant section of the proposal |
| Has the team delivered comparable work? | Live example and explanation of its contribution |
| Are the people and responsibilities clear? | Named lead and responsibility list |
| Can we supply the required inputs? | Content and approval schedule |
| Is quality review defined? | Acceptance checklist and issue-resolution process |
| Can we operate and leave the arrangement? | Access, documentation, licensing, and exit terms |
| Can we afford the complete arrangement? | Initial, recurring, optional, and internal costs |

Choose your nonnegotiable requirements before scoring preferences. A beautiful portfolio should not outweigh an unresolved essential integration. Use the worksheet to locate missing information, then make a judgment; a numerical total cannot make incompatible proposals equivalent.

## A hypothetical comparison

Imagine a property-maintenance company replacing an unclear service website. It has good photographs, limited writing capacity, and an existing scheduling tool it wants to keep.

Agency A proposes a distinctive design but assumes the company will supply finished copy. Agency B includes interviews and writing but excludes the scheduling connection. Agency C offers a simpler visual treatment, content assistance, and a demonstrated connection to the existing tool.

The company should ask A to price the missing writing and B to clarify the integration before deciding. C may fit best, but only after its editing workflow, testing, and support terms are understood. The decision follows the company's constraints, not a universal ranking of agencies.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many agencies should we speak with?

Speak with enough credible candidates to compare approaches without creating more evaluation work than your team can handle. A small, relevant shortlist is usually easier to assess than a large collection of unrelated proposals. There is no required number.

### Should an agency provide a free design before we decide?

A speculative design can be attractive without addressing the underlying requirements. Ask for relevant examples and a clear proposed process. If substantial investigation is needed, consider a separately scoped discovery engagement with defined outputs you can keep.

### Is the lowest proposal a bad choice?

No. It may reflect a narrower scope or an efficient approach that suits you. Check what you must supply, what happens after launch, and which requirements are excluded. Compare the complete arrangement before deciding whether the price is good value.

## Prepare the brief for your next conversation

Write down the main problem, your essential requirements, and the decisions still open. Use those notes to ask more useful questions of every provider you consider.
