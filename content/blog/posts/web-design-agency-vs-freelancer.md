A freelancer and an agency can both deliver a useful business website. The labels do not tell you who will write the content, test the forms, manage the launch, or help when your site needs attention later.

The practical choice is between two proposed delivery arrangements. One may give you direct access to an experienced generalist. Another may coordinate several specialists. Either arrangement can work well when its responsibilities match the project.

Before choosing, map the work and decide how much coordination your own team can take on. That gives you a better comparison than assumptions about who must be cheaper, faster, or more reliable.

## Understand the actual team behind each proposal

Ask every provider to describe who will do the work. A freelance designer may partner with a developer and writer. An agency may assign one person to most of the project and use outside specialists for particular tasks.

Neither setup is inherently a problem. You need to know who is accountable for the finished result, who communicates with you, and whether additional participants are included in the quoted scope.

Useful questions include:

- Who leads design, development, writing, and testing?
- Which responsibilities will our business retain?
- Who coordinates any specialists or subcontractors?
- Who can approve changes to the scope and schedule?
- Will the people shown in the proposal be available for this project?

Treat an unnamed future resource as an open question. A list of capabilities on a company website is not the same as a commitment to provide them on your project.

## Match the delivery model to the work

A focused project with ready content and a clear approval owner may suit a single capable provider. A project involving several departments, substantial content development, and multiple integrations needs an explicit coordination plan, whatever the supplier calls itself.

Use this comparison to shape the conversation:

| Project condition | What to look for in either arrangement |
| --- | --- |
| Scope is focused and content is ready | Relevant implementation skills and a clear delivery sequence |
| Writing, photography, and design are all needed | Named responsibility for each discipline |
| Several people must approve the site | A method for consolidating feedback and resolving disagreements |
| The site connects to operational tools | Technical ownership across the connection and failure handling |
| Your team cannot manage the project closely | Coordination included as an actual deliverable |
| A critical service needs ongoing attention | Support coverage and escalation terms that match that need |

Avoid adding complexity just to justify a larger team. Equally, avoid making your staff the unplanned project manager for work they cannot realistically coordinate.

## Count the coordination you will provide

Two proposals can have similar deliverables and very different expectations of the client. One provider may gather feedback and manage content deadlines. Another may expect you to supply final copy, coordinate photography, and collect every approval.

Write down the weekly decisions your team will make. Who checks factual accuracy? Who decides between conflicting suggestions? Who approves a change that affects both the budget and launch date?

Direct access to a freelancer can make discussion straightforward. It does not remove the need to document decisions. A project manager can provide a clear point of contact, but that role is useful only if questions reach the people who can answer them.

Ask to see a sample project update with confidential details removed. Look for completed work, decisions needed, risks, and next steps. You are assessing whether the communication would help your team act, rather than how elaborate the reporting looks.

## Evaluate availability and continuity explicitly

Do not assume an agency has automatic backup coverage or that a freelancer cannot provide continuity. Ask how the particular arrangement works during leave, illness, competing commitments, or a change in personnel.

A backup person must have the knowledge and authorized access needed to help. A name on a contact list is insufficient if the site can only be deployed from someone else's computer.

For both options, clarify:

- Expected working availability and planned absence during the project.
- How a delay is communicated and the schedule is revised.
- Who can address an urgent issue after launch.
- Where project decisions and operating instructions are documented.
- What happens if the relationship ends before or after completion.

Your business also needs continuity. Give a second appropriate person access to essential account information and the project records. Keep a clear internal owner, even when a supplier manages the technical work.

## Separate access, ownership, and portability

These three questions are often compressed into “Will we own the site?” Ask them separately.

**Access:** Can your authorized staff administer the relevant accounts and publish the changes they need? Access should match responsibilities. WordPress, for example, distinguishes roles such as Editor and Administrator; being able to edit content does not mean someone controls every site setting. [WordPress roles and capabilities](https://wordpress.org/documentation/article/roles-and-capabilities/).

**Ownership and licensing:** Which original deliverables are assigned to your business, and which elements use third-party licenses? Get the agreed terms for code, design files, fonts, photographs, and purchased components in writing. Do not assume the provider's business structure answers those questions.

**Portability:** What would a replacement provider need to operate the site? A source-code copy is only part of the answer when the site depends on a database, external service, or deployment configuration.

As a concrete example, WordPress documents that a typical complete restoration requires both files and a database backup. A content export or a theme folder alone should not be described as a complete recovery package. [WordPress backup documentation](https://developer.wordpress.org/advanced-administration/security/backup/).

## Compare costs with the same responsibilities included

There is no useful rule that a freelancer always costs less or that an agency's higher fee always buys more coverage. Compare actual proposals, including the work your team must supply.

Record the initial fee, recurring services, optional work, and likely change requests. Add an estimate of internal time for writing, approvals, coordination, and administration. Keep internal time separate from supplier invoices so you can see both cash requirements and workload.

Ask how estimates change when assumptions fail. If the existing site's content is harder to migrate than expected, who investigates and authorizes the extra work? If you pause the project while waiting for photographs, what happens to the booked schedule?

For ongoing support, compare specific inclusions. Software updates, content edits, uptime monitoring, and feature development are different responsibilities. A monthly fee or an hourly rate is meaningful only alongside the work it covers.

Then run a handover exercise on paper: the original provider is unavailable and you need to update a service and repair a failed inquiry notification. Identify who would act, what they would need, and where that information would be found. Any missing step is a question to resolve now.

## A hypothetical decision with two reasonable answers

Imagine a local consulting firm with approved copy, an established booking tool, and one director who can make prompt decisions. An experienced freelancer proposes a focused build, documented handover, and an optional support arrangement. An agency proposes a broader discovery and content process.

The freelancer may fit because the firm already has the inputs the broader process would produce. The agency's additional work is not automatically valuable in that situation.

Now change the facts: three departments disagree about the services, the copy is unfinished, and nobody can coordinate the project internally. An agency proposal that explicitly includes research, writing, and decision management may fit better. A freelancer offering those same capabilities through a managed team could also be suitable.

The decision changes because the responsibilities change. Neither label wins by default.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an agency safer if our contact leaves?

Only if the arrangement provides practical continuity. Ask who else knows the project, where records live, and how access is maintained. An organization name does not prove that another person can take over successfully.

### Can a freelancer handle a complex website?

Possibly. Look for relevant experience, realistic capacity, and a clear plan for any specialist work. Complexity needs to be explained in the scope; it should not be inferred from page count or dismissed because one person leads the project.

### Can we move to another provider later?

That depends on the platform, licensing, access, documentation, and agreed handover terms. Ask for a specific exit explanation before signing. Moving may involve work even when you have full access to the existing site.

## Choose the arrangement your team can sustain

Compare responsibility maps before comparing labels. Select the proposal that covers the work you need, fits your participation, and leaves you with a workable operating arrangement.
